### 1. Mathematical Reasoning

**Direct conceptual links:** Inferred rather than explicit. Mathematical reasoning appears in the AI literacy corpus primarily because the *population* studied is mathematics teachers or mathematics education contexts — not because AI literacy frameworks have incorporated mathematical reasoning as a competency.

**Representative publications:**

- **Zhang, Wijaya & Wang (2025)** — "Exploring the relationship between AI literacy, AI trust, AI dependency, and 21st century skills in preservice mathematics teachers" (*Scientific Reports*, 41 citations, OA). The most-cited paper with a direct population connection: measures AI literacy in mathematics teacher trainees and correlates it with 21st-century skills. Mathematical reasoning is implicit in "21st century skills" but not operationalized as a distinct AI literacy component. The 41 citing papers (ScholeXplorer) concentrate on AI dependency/trust, not on mathematical reasoning in AI literacy — confirming the paper is read through the AI dependency frame, not the math education frame.
- **Cevikbas & Kaiser (2026)** — "LLMs to solve mathematical modeling problems: preservice mathematics teachers' use practices, perceived affordances, challenges, and trustworthiness judgments" (*ZDM – Mathematics Education*, 4 citations). Explicit: addresses the judgment dimension — how teacher trainees evaluate and trust AI-generated mathematical reasoning. Bridges AI literacy and mathematical thinking at the epistemic level. Published in a mathematics education journal (*ZDM*), not an AI literacy or educational technology journal, indicating disciplinary location is mathematics education.
- **Davor, Boateng & Osei-Tutu (2026)** — "Mediating Roles of Digital Literacy and Critical Thinking in ChatGPT Use and Mathematical Problem-Solving Efficacy" (3 citations). Adjacent: treats AI/digital literacy as mediating variable for math outcomes, not as an object of study in its own right.
- **Subroto, Malik & Raditya (2024)** — "A bibliometric analysis on artificial intelligence in mathematics education" (*JRAMATHEDU*, 11 citations). Adjacent: maps what AI research exists in math education but does not theorize AI literacy or mathematical reasoning as a competency.

**Explicit vs. inferred:** The link is almost entirely inferred. No paper in the OpenAIRE corpus defines "mathematical reasoning" as a component of AI literacy. The connection appears in population choice (mathematics teachers as research subjects) and in adjacent calls for integration, not in competency-framework design.

**Bibliometric class:** find\_by\_influence\_class C3 for "AI literacy mathematical reasoning" returned results dominated by general AI literacy papers (Long & Magerko, Ng et al.) — confirming no high-influence papers are specifically at this intersection. The highest-citation paper with genuine relevance is Zhang et al. (2025) at 41 citations, which is C4 territory.

### 2. Mathematical Literacy

**Direct conceptual links:** Almost none. The 138 records for "AI literacy mathematical literacy education" are dominated by generic AI-in-education papers where "literacy" and "mathematics" and "AI" co-occur incidentally. The mathematics literacy tradition (Niss, PISA's mathematical literacy definition) does not appear to have a direct line into AI literacy framework development.

**The data literacy bridge (strongest adjacent link):** The most important finding at this concept is the explicit mention of **data literacy** within AI literacy frameworks. **Chiu et al. (2024)** — "What are artificial intelligence literacy and competency? A comprehensive framework to support them" (*Computers and Education Open*, **358 citations**, C3 influence, C2 popularity, C1 impulse) — is the field's current highest-impact comprehensive framework paper. Its abstract explicitly identifies five future research directions: prompt engineering, **data literacy**, algorithmic literacy, self-reflective mindset, and empirical research. Data literacy overlaps with quantitative/mathematical literacy in its concern with interpreting, evaluating, and reasoning about data. This represents the **strongest counter-evidence** and the clearest pathway toward integration — but Chiu et al. position data literacy as a *future direction*, not a present component. The recognition of the gap is itself evidence that mathematical-quantitative reasoning is not yet integrated.

**Bibliometric class:** Chiu et al. (2024) is the one C3-class paper in the vicinity of this intersection, but it addresses data literacy aspirationally, not as documented research.

### 3. Model-Based Reasoning

**Direct conceptual links:** Moderate — this is the least underspecified of the six concepts within the AI literacy corpus. Understanding how ML models make decisions appears in AI literacy frameworks (Long & Magerko's "How AI Works" competencies; AIUK's "Representing and Reasoning" principle) and has begun generating dedicated empirical research.

**Representative publications:**

- **Dong, Jiang & Xu (2025)** — "From simplification to sophistication: Secondary students' conceptual change in understanding machine learning model decision making" (*BJET*, 2 citations). The most explicit paper at this intersection: traces how secondary students develop mental models of ML decision-making over time, identifying a progression from naive simplification (treating ML as human-like thinking) to mechanistic sophistication. This is directly about model-based reasoning development. Published in *BJET* — within AI literacy's core publishing venues.
- **Kajiwara et al. (2023)** — "Machine learning role playing game: Instructional design of AI education for age-appropriate in K-12 and beyond" (*CAEAI*, 14 citations). Adjacent: uses a role-play game to teach ML concepts, including model behavior and prediction — touches model-based reasoning pedagogically without theorizing it explicitly.

**Explicit vs. inferred:** The link is *structurally present* in AI literacy frameworks (model understanding is a recognized competency) and *empirically nascent* (Dong et al. 2025 is among the first papers to study it as a developmental process). The concept is in the AI literacy canon but not yet a developed sub-field.

**Cluster status:** No stable cluster — 14 total records, 0 high-citation papers specifically on model-based reasoning as an AI literacy competency. This is where the field is most clearly heading, but the empirical base is not yet there.

### 5. Probabilistic Reasoning

**Direct conceptual links:** The most substantively absent of all six concepts — but also the site of the most interesting emergent work.

**Foundational paper:** **How & Hung (2019)** — "Educing AI-Thinking in Science, Technology, Engineering, Arts, and Mathematics (STEAM) Education" (*Education Sciences*, **107 citations**, OA; DOI: `10.3390/educsci9030184`). This is the only paper in the OpenAIRE corpus that proposes a pedagogical framework using Bayesian/probabilistic reasoning as a mechanism for developing human inferential skills through AI. The concept "AI-Thinking" positions AI not just as a tool but as a cognitive scaffold: by working with AI systems that generate probabilistic outputs, students develop Bayesian-style reasoning. This was published six years ago and has 107 citations.

**Citation network analysis (40 citing papers):** The crucial finding is that the 40 papers citing How & Hung are citing it for its **STEAM/AI education framing**, not specifically for its Bayesian probabilistic reasoning pedagogy. Representative citing papers include:

- "Conceptualizing AI literacy: An exploratory review" (Kong et al., *CAEAI*)
- "Integration of Artificial Intelligence in K-12"
- "Mapping the Landscape of AI Implementation in STEM and STEAM Education"
- STEAM education bibliometric analyses

None of the 40 citing papers develop or empirically test the Bayesian reasoning dimension of AI-Thinking. The probabilistic reasoning angle has not generated a follow-on research stream.

**Nascent empirical work:** **Pramartha, Hendriyanto & Muhaimin (2026)** — "AI-thinking in secondary mathematics education: A design-based research on Bayesian-based probabilistic reasoning" (*Indonesian Journal of Science and Mathematics Education*, 0 citations). This is the only paper directly developing Bayesian probabilistic reasoning in the context of AI-Thinking and mathematics education. It explicitly builds on How & Hung (2019). With 0 citations at time of writing, it marks the beginning of what could become a sub-stream, but the stream does not yet exist.

**Record count for "AI literacy probabilistic reasoning uncertainty":** **6** — the smallest count of any concept pair tested across all three investigations. Trend data: 9 total papers 2018–2025, with 7 in 2025 alone.

**Explicit vs. inferred:** How & Hung is explicit in proposing Bayesian AI-Thinking; Pramartha et al. is explicit in applying it. All other connections are inferred.

### 6. Uncertainty

**Direct conceptual links:** Two modes of connection, both peripheral:

- *Uncertainty about AI outputs* (epistemological): How do users reason about confidence, reliability, and fallibility of AI-generated content? This is close to AI literacy's "critical evaluation" competency but is rarely framed as statistical/probabilistic uncertainty.
- *Uncertainty quantification* (technical): The body of work on uncertainty estimation in ML models is entirely technical and does not intersect with AI literacy education.

**Representative publications:** Córdova & Nóbrega (2025) is again the most explicit — arguing that uncertainty in AI outputs requires users to have statistical thinking skills to interpret confidence intervals, error rates, and probabilistic claims. No other papers in the OpenAIRE corpus systematically address how uncertainty reasoning should be incorporated into AI literacy education.

**Bibliometric class:** The "uncertainty quantification AI systems education training" query (16 records) returned papers on technical ML uncertainty (neutron diffusion modeling, neural networks) — entirely off-topic. There is no educational AI literacy research on uncertainty reasoning reaching C3 influence.

**Explicit vs. inferred:** The connection is inferred from first principles (AI outputs are probabilistic; literacy requires interpreting them), advocated explicitly in one domain-specific paper (medicine), and otherwise absent from the AI literacy research corpus.

### Disciplinary Cluster Analysis

**Does a stable research cluster exist?** No. The evidence for this conclusion is structural:

1. **No shared citation base:** The papers at each intersection point to different literatures — How & Hung (2019) in STEAM, Chiu (2024) in comprehensive AI literacy frameworks, Cevikbas & Kaiser in mathematics education, Córdova & Nóbrega in medicine. They do not cite each other.
2. **No recurring authors:** No author appears across two or more of the six sub-intersections. Thomas Chiu is closest to a bridge figure (mentions data literacy in his framework), but his empirical work does not address quantitative or probabilistic reasoning.
3. **No dedicated journal venue:** Papers are scattered across *Education Sciences*, *ZDM*, *BJET*, *CAEAI*, *Numeracy*, and a psychology journal. No conference or special issue appears dedicated to this intersection.
4. **No funded projects:** OpenAIRE project search returned 0 results for "AI literacy mathematical reasoning education."
5. **Temporal distribution:** 90%+ of papers appeared in 2024–2025. Embryonic activity, not a stable field.

**What does exist instead:** Three disconnected currents that could eventually constitute a cluster if cross-citation develops:

- The **AI-Thinking tradition** (How & Hung 2019; Pramartha 2026) — probabilistic reasoning via AI in STEAM, Singapore/Indonesian contexts
- The **mathematics education + LLM tradition** (Cevikbas & Kaiser 2026, *ZDM*) — mathematics education scholarship engaging with AI judgment
- The **quantitative literacy response** (Karaali 2023, *Numeracy*) — QL/QR community arguing for quantitative reasoning in response to AI

These three currents share the conclusion that quantitative/probabilistic reasoning becomes *more* important in an AI-saturated environment, but they are not yet in dialogue with each other and do not share the AI literacy framework vocabulary.

### Counter-Evidence: Where Integration Already Exists or Is Developing

The claim that mathematical/probabilistic reasoning is underdeveloped in AI literacy research requires qualification by the following counter-evidence:

**1. Chiu et al. (2024) explicitly calls for data literacy integration** (358 citations, C3 influence, C1 impulse). The field's leading current comprehensive framework explicitly names data literacy as a future research direction for AI literacy. This is not evidence that integration is complete — it is evidence that the field's leading researchers have identified the gap and are directing the field toward it. The trajectory is toward integration.

**2. How & Hung (2019) established a probabilistic reasoning AI pedagogy six years ago** (107 citations). The AI-Thinking framework is not new; it is established and cited. The gap is that this framework has not been adopted by the AI literacy mainstream — it circulates in STEAM education but is not incorporated into the Ng/Long-and-Magerko/Chiu definitional tradition.

**3. The Long & Magerko (2020) framework includes "How AI Works" competencies** that structurally require probabilistic reasoning (supervised/unsupervised learning, representation, training), even if not named as such. A student who understands how a neural network learns from data implicitly needs some probabilistic intuition. The competency infrastructure is present; empirical research on whether students acquire probabilistic reasoning from AI literacy instruction is absent.

**4. AIUK/AI4K12's Five Big Ideas** include "Representing and Reasoning" — explicitly probabilistic in conception (Bayesian networks, probabilistic inference). This is a curriculum-level integration that predates the peer-reviewed AI literacy literature, which has been slow to conduct empirical research on whether students develop probabilistic reasoning through AI literacy curricula built on this standard.

**5. Karaali (2023) in *****Numeracy*** argues that quantitative literacy and AI literacy are complementary responses to the same epistemic challenge — AI's encroachment on quantitative tasks elevates, rather than diminishes, the importance of human quantitative reasoning. This is the clearest theoretical argument for integration, published in the quantitative literacy community's own journal.

The overarching finding: every mathematical/statistical/probabilistic concept connects to AI literacy through advocacy, structural logic, or population choice — but none has generated a body of empirical research that would constitute a recognizable sub-field.
