## AI Literacy Research: A Map of the Field

### The Growth Curve

The field is experiencing one of the sharpest expansions in recent education research. OpenAIRE indexes **12,921 publications** matching "AI literacy" between 2018 and 2025 — a **224-fold increase** in seven years. The growth is clearly bimodal: a first wave (2018–2022) of foundational definition-building (\~40 → \~280 papers/year), followed by a second, ChatGPT-driven explosion from 2023 onward (721 → 8,979 papers/year). The two phases have meaningfully different research characters.

---

### The Two Anchor Papers — and Why They Matter

The entire field can be triangulated from two papers that function as shared reference points across nearly every subsequent publication:

**1. Long & Magerko (2020) — "What is AI Literacy? Competencies and Design Considerations"**

- Published at ACM CHI 2020 | DOI: `10.1145/3313831.3376727`
- **1,975 citations** | OpenAIRE metrics: **C2 influence** (top 0.1%, field-normalized), **C1 popularity** (top 0.01% in current citation velocity)
- ScholeXplorer records **2,099 papers** citing it — the broadest citation reach in the field
- Defines **17 core competencies** of AI literacy (ranging from "Recognizing AI" and "Understanding ML" to "Critical appraisal of AI" and "Designing AI with ethics"), organized around five thematic clusters derived from the K-12 initiative *AI4K12*'s "Five Big Ideas in AI." Also proposes design considerations for learner-centered AI systems, bridging the HCI and education communities.
- Methodologically: a synthesis of interdisciplinary literature rather than empirical work — its value is definitional and organizational.

**2. Ng, Leung & Chu (2021) — "Conceptualizing AI literacy: An exploratory review"**

- Published in *Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence* | DOI: `10.1016/j.caeai.2021.100041`
- **1,241 citations** | OpenAIRE metrics: **C2 influence**, **C1 popularity**, gold open access
- ScholeXplorer records **1,249 citing papers**
- Proposes a **four-aspect model** of AI literacy grounded in an analogy with classical literacy models: **(i) Know & Understand** (conceptual knowledge of AI), **(ii) Use & Apply** (functional use of AI tools), **(iii) Evaluate & Create** (critical assessment and making with AI), and **(iv) Ethical issues** (responsible use). This four-part structure became the backbone of most subsequent frameworks.
- Cites Joseph Aoun's *Robot-Proof* (MIT Press, 2017, 386 citations) as an intellectual ancestor — the book's argument for "humanics" (AI-complementary literacies for a robot-proof education) is where the field's political economy framing originates.

The two papers co-exist as **complementary anchors**: Long & Magerko from the HCI/design perspective (competency-centered, learner experience, informal learning), Ng et al. from the educational sciences perspective (literacy theory, structured dimensions, teaching implications). Virtually every subsequent framework — Chiu's 2024 synthesis, Laupichler's scoping reviews, Casal-Otero's K-12 review — cites both.

### Nine Research Strands

#### 1. Conceptual and Definitional Frameworks *(founding strand, 2019–2022)*

The field's primary activity in its early years was answering "what *is* AI literacy?" Papers in this strand include Ng et al.'s ASIS&T companion piece — **"AI Literacy: Definition, Teaching, Evaluation and Ethical Issues"** (2021, 381 citations, DOI: `10.1002/pra2.487`) — and the most recent synthesis: **Chiu, Ahmad & Ismailov (2024)** — *"What are artificial intelligence literacy and competency? A comprehensive framework"* (358 citations, DOI: `10.1016/j.caeo.2024.100171`), which tries to unify the diverging conceptual vocabularies and align AI literacy with competency-based education frameworks. A parallel concern in this strand is distinguishing AI literacy from adjacent constructs: **data literacy, digital literacy, algorithm literacy, and computational thinking** — the latter being a frequent predecessor cited in K-12 contexts.

#### 2. K-12 AI Education *(2021–present, high growth)*

This strand operationalizes AI literacy as a curriculum design problem for primary and secondary education. The dominant paper is **Casal-Otero, Catala & Fernández-Morante (2023)** — *"AI literacy in K-12: a systematic literature review"* (498 citations, *International Journal of STEM Education*, DOI: `10.1186/s40594-023-00418-7`), which maps 135 studies across age groups, curriculum approaches, and geographic contexts. The strand connects closely to **culturally responsive pedagogy**: Eguchi, Okada & Muto's (2021) work on contextualizing AI education for K-12 students across cultures (144 citations, *KI – Künstliche Intelligenz*) is a key node. The AI4K12 initiative (US, NSF-funded) and "Day of AI" curriculum are the institutional backdrops, though these live outside OpenAIRE's indexed corpus. Long's more recent work also lives here — museum exhibits, hands-on "unplugged" AI activities for young learners (e.g., "Introducing AI Without Computers", 2025).

#### 3. Higher Education and Adult Learners *(2022–present)*

**Laupichler, Aster & Schirch (2022)** — *"AI literacy in higher and adult education: a scoping review"* (415 citations, *Computers and Education: AI*, DOI: `10.1016/j.caeai.2022.100101`) is the anchor. It maps the state of AI literacy in post-secondary contexts, noting a heavy imbalance toward STEM and medical disciplines and a relative neglect of humanities and social sciences. **Southworth et al. (2023)** — *"AI Across the Curriculum: transforming higher education"* (394 citations, DOI: `10.1016/j.caeai.2023.100127`) offers a practical curricular model embedding AI literacy across all disciplines rather than confining it to CS courses. This strand is strongly influenced by the **career readiness framing** inherited from Aoun's *Robot-Proof* — the idea that AI literacy is a labor-market imperative, not just an educational aspiration.

#### 4. Generative AI Literacy *(2023–present, fastest-growing strand)*

The most recent and fastest-growing strand emerged directly from the public availability of ChatGPT (November 2022). **Walter (2024)** — *"Embracing the future of AI in the classroom: AI literacy, prompt engineering, and critical thinking"* (786 citations in under two years, *International Journal of Educational Technology in Higher Education*, DOI: `10.1186/s41239-024-00448-3`) is the most-cited new entrant. It repositions AI literacy around three pillars: foundational conceptual knowledge, **prompt engineering as a practical skill**, and **critical thinking as a meta-competency** for evaluating generative outputs. **Annapureddy, Fornaroli & Gatica-Perez (2025)** — *"Generative AI Literacy: Twelve Defining Competencies"* (108 citations, *Digital Government*, DOI: `10.1145/3685680`) extends Long & Magerko's competency architecture specifically to LLM-era tools. **Chan & Lee (2023)** — *"The AI generation gap"* (491 citations, *Smart Learning Environments*) introduces a generational dimension, finding Gen Z students more open to GenAI adoption than their Gen X or millennial teachers — an important empirical finding driving teacher-side interventions. The broader "ChatGPT in higher education" literature (Dempere et al., 2023, 391 citations; Chiu's 2024 future-research agenda, 332 citations) is adjacent and cross-cited, though it is not all *about* AI literacy per se.

#### 5. Teacher AI Competency and Professional Development *(2023–present)*

As AI literacy became a curriculum goal, a parallel question arose: do teachers have the AI literacy to teach it? **Thomas K.F. Chiu** is the dominant voice here. His most theoretically original contribution is the **Intelligent-TPACK (I-TPACK) framework** (2025) — an extension of Mishra & Koehler's classic Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge framework to include AI knowledge as a distinct dimension teachers must develop. His 2024 comprehensive framework (358 citations) is widely used as a professional development blueprint. **Fundi, Sanusi & Oyelere (2024)** — *"Advancing AI education: Assessing Kenyan in-service teachers' preparedness"* (56 citations) documents the significant readiness gap in Global South contexts, a geographic lacuna the field is beginning to address.

#### 6. AI Ethics Education *(2021–present)*

This strand argues that AI literacy is fundamentally incomplete without explicit ethics instruction. **Kwak, Ahn & Seo (2022)** — *"Influence of AI ethics awareness, attitude, anxiety, and self-efficacy on nursing students' behavioral intentions"* (218 citations, *BMC Nursing*) is the most-cited entry, reflecting the strand's strong foothold in health professions education. **Usher & Barak (2024)** — *"Unpacking the role of AI ethics online education for science and engineering students"* (77 citations, *International Journal of STEM Education*) provides a curriculum design perspective. **Ma, Ng & Liu (2025)** — *"Fostering responsible AI literacy: a systematic review of K-12 AI ethics education"* (26 citations, DOI: `10.1016/j.caeai.2025.100422`) represents the newest synthesis and directly extends Ng et al.'s original four-aspect model by treating ethics not as a peripheral concern but as a structural element of the literacy construct. A companion paper by Ng et al. (2025) — *"Why don't teachers teach AI ethics?"* — empirically investigates implementation barriers through the Theory of Planned Behaviour.

#### 7. Assessment and Measurement *(2022–present)*

A methodologically distinct strand building psychometric instruments. **Matthias Carl Laupichler** (University of Bonn) is the most productive author here. His *"Evaluating AI Courses"* paper (2023, 41 citations, DOI: `10.3390/educsci13100978`) presents a validated comparative self-assessment instrument for AI course outcomes. His doctoral thesis (*"Are they lit?"*, 2024) is the most comprehensive psychometric treatment of AI literacy measurement. **Koch, Carolus & Wienrich (2024)** — *"Meta AI literacy scale: Further validation and development of a short version"* (20 citations, *Heliyon*, DOI: `10.1016/j.heliyon.2024.e39686`) provides a brief instrument usable in large-scale surveys. The strand faces a persistent definitional challenge: because researchers disagree on what AI literacy *is*, their measurement instruments assess different things, making cross-study comparison difficult. Biagini (2024) in *Media Education* makes this critique explicit in a multidimensional assessment review.

#### 8. Domain-Specific AI Literacy *(2022–present)*

This strand asks: what does AI literacy look like for doctors, journalists, engineers, nurses, or pilots? The framing shifts from generic competency models to **situated, professional literacies**. Eguchi et al.'s culturally responsive K-12 work is adjacent; **Kabashkin et al. (2023)** address aviation professionals; healthcare has the largest body of work (nursing, dentistry, medical education). Importantly, the Long & Magerko framework is being cited and partially adapted in each domain rather than fully reconstructed — confirming the founding papers' role as a cross-cutting infrastructure.

#### 9. AI-Human Collaboration and Learner Experience Design *(2021–present)*

The most HCI-oriented strand, addressing how learning environments should be designed to build AI literacy through interaction. **Kim, Lee & Cho (2022)** — *"Learning design to support student-AI collaboration"* (478 citations, *Education and Information Technologies*, DOI: `10.1007/s10639-021-10831-6`) examines how teachers conceptualize AI as a collaborator in student learning. Duri Long's more recent work — *"Designing Interactive Explainable AI Tools for Algorithmic Literacy and Transparency"* (28 citations, 2024), *"BiasViz: A Project-Based Tool for Engaging Middle School Students in Critical Thinking about AI Biases"* (2026), and *"AI Unplugged"* (2026) — is developing a body of **interactive, embodied AI literacy tools** grounded in learning sciences and HCI design principles.

### Key Concepts and Their Connections

```
Robot-Proof (Aoun, 2017) → "humanics" as AI-age literacy goal
        ↓
Digital literacy / Media literacy / Data literacy ← ancestors cited in foundations
        ↓
[2020] Long & Magerko: 17 competencies, 5 Big Ideas → HCI design considerations
[2021] Ng et al.: Know/Use/Evaluate/Ethics → teaching/assessment framework
        ↓
        ├── K-12 curriculum design (computational thinking, unplugged, AI4K12)
        ├── Higher education scoping (Laupichler, Southworth)
        ├── Teacher competency / I-TPACK (Chiu)
        ├── Ethics-first framing (Ma, Ng; Usher & Barak)
        ├── Measurement instruments (Laupichler, Koch)
        └── Domain-specific adaptations (healthcare, journalism, aviation)
                ↓
        [2023–] ChatGPT inflection
                ├── Generative AI literacy (Walter; Annapureddy)
                ├── Prompt engineering as competency
                ├── Critical thinking against AI output
                └── GenAI adoption gaps (Chan & Lee generational divide)

```

Three conceptual tensions run through the field:

1. **Breadth vs. depth**: Should AI literacy be a universal thin layer (everyone knows the five big ideas) or a deep competency (professionals who can evaluate, audit, and design AI)? Chiu's 2024 framework tries to resolve this with a tiered model distinguishing *literacy* (broad, essential) from *competency* (domain-specific, advanced).
2. **Technical vs. critical**: Early frameworks (Long & Magerko) emphasize conceptual understanding of *how* AI works; critical AI literacy scholars (referencing Freire and media literacy traditions) insist the more important question is *who* AI benefits and *what* power relationships it reinforces. The critical strand is less dominant in citation counts but growing.
3. **Definition stability**: The field still lacks a fully consensual definition. Ng et al.'s four aspects, Long & Magerko's 17 competencies, and Chiu's framework use partially overlapping but non-identical vocabularies — a problem the measurement strand makes vivid, since instruments operationalize different definitions.
